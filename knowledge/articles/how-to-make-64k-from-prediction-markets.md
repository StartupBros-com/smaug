This article frames prediction markets as systems for price discovery, liquidity, and information transmission rather than as simple bets. It surveys eleven potential approaches: comparing prices across venues, supplying liquidity to automated market maker pools, reacting to news faster than slower market participants, interpreting an event’s resolution source, treating contracts as binary options, and using market attention as a secondary trading signal.

The strongest practical theme is that an apparent edge requires more than identifying an idea: fees, liquidity, execution speed, settlement rules, position sizing, legal restrictions, and counterparty risk can eliminate it. The article also describes attempts to influence outcomes and tax strategies; those ideas carry serious ethical, legal, and regulatory concerns and should not be treated as trading advice.

## Key Takeaways

- Cross-market price differences and delayed updates can create opportunities only when costs, limits, and execution risk remain lower than the apparent spread.
- Liquidity provision in automated market maker pools resembles a volatility-sensitive strategy: fees compensate for inventory and adverse-selection risks rather than constituting free yield.
- Market odds can serve as an information feed for related assets or narratives, but correlations can fail and fast-moving news can reverse a trade before it is executable.
- Binary contracts can be analyzed with option-like concepts such as implied probability, payoff asymmetry, and time remaining, but their settlement terms are critical.
- Resolution sources, market rules, jurisdiction, and ethical constraints must be understood before capital is committed; the article’s more aggressive proposals deserve particular skepticism.
