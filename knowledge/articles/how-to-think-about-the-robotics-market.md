Unsigned RoboStrategy Advisors primer (published via @intern) arguing that VLMs just gave robots a brain, so the addressable market is no longer "industrial automation" but the ~$60T global labor pool. A $50k humanoid working 20 hours/day for 7 years, plus $5k/year maintenance and power, pencils at ~$1.66/hour — cheap enough that the economics still work if the true number is $3–5. Today's private robotics book is roughly $200B ($100–400B depending on whether you count Waymo/Anduril), so filling the labor TAM would be a ~300x, Bitcoin-from-$200-to-$60k style move. Not investment advice; the piece is framed as education from an adviser focused on robotics and physical AI.

The bottom-up cut is more concrete than the top-down 300x: Walmart and Amazon each employ 1M+ labor workers at $20–40+/hour. Two million $50k robots sold to just those two buyers is $100B of hardware revenue; 10M units is $500B; 100M is $5T — already larger than any single company's annual revenue. Game theory then does the rest: once one competitor cuts unit labor cost that far, non-adopters get margin-compressed out. Jevons (cheaper labor → more demand for physical work) is treated as upside, not required for the thesis.

The more important modeling choice is sell vs lease. Near-term the constraint is supply, not demand — if you can only build 1M robots against 10M of demand, you keep the fleet and sell the labor, the way GPU clouds sell compute. A robot leased at ~$50k/year over a 7-year life is $350k of lifetime revenue instead of a one-time $50k hardware sale. Winners then look less like hardware OEMs and more like owners of scarce, cash-flowing labor fleets.

Scaling is flagged as the least-clear variable. The piece bets it happens faster than consensus because of four stacked accelerants: private capital already pouring in, talent gravitating to a high-upside problem, robots eventually building robots, and a national arms race. On the last, it cites FCC Public Notice DA 26-786 (July 28, 2026) moving to block imported foreign humanoid and quadruped robots, and expects a US National Robotics Strategy — because a lead in physical AI is a path to GDP, defense, and domestic-production dominance, and no government wants a rival's robot in its homes and factories.

## Key Takeaways

- The inflection is the brain, not the body: AI that can take messy real-world inputs and produce intelligent physical outputs is what turned centuries of science fiction into a product
- Illustrative all-in robot labor cost is ~$1.66/hour ($50k unit, 20h × 365 × 7 years = 51,100 hours, plus $35k of maintenance/power). The thesis survives large errors in that number
- Top-down TAM is global labor (~52% of $118T GDP ≈ $60T) vs a ~$200B private robotics market; bottom-up, just Amazon + Walmart is already a $100B hardware order if they swap 2M workers
- Leasing the labor (not selling the robot) can 7x lifetime revenue per unit and turns the winner into a fleet operator, not an OEM
- Supply, not demand, is the near-term bottleneck; a national-priority / FCC-import-ban overlay is the geopolitical accelerant
- Scaling timeline is the weakest part of the argument; "robots building robots" and a US National Robotics Strategy are the proposed fixes, not current facts
