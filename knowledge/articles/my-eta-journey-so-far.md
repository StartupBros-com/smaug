A two-year retrospective on buying and operating a manufacturing company after leaving Wall Street at age 40. The author acquired the business for roughly 3.75x including inventory with no prior operating experience, then encountered the unglamorous reality of being the sole manager: learning aging factory equipment, quoting jobs, scheduling production, managing freight, paying vendors, and invoicing customers.

The first year brought operational shocks that the acquisition thesis did not erase: two damaging maintenance hires, a resin-delivery interruption that forced $50,000 in emergency purchases, an approximately 15% demand decline during a manufacturing recession, and borrowing costs that rose as prime increased. The account treats those events as evidence that personal guarantees, family constraints, and limited control over customers or macro conditions make ETA unsuitable for most prospective buyers.

Several choices helped offset those pressures. The author extended major vendor terms from net 30 to net 60 while customers remained net 30, reduced some raw-material costs despite inflation, retained most of the team, and used a capital structure of 20% equity, 20% fixed-rate family-and-friends debt, and 60% SBA 7(a) financing. Salary, cash accumulation, and debt paydown produced a reported 1.6x return on equity over two years, alongside a better family routine than the prior finance career.

## Key Takeaways

- Underwrite acquisitions for at least 2.0x debt-service coverage rather than accepting the 1.25–1.50x range some lenders will finance.
- Keep debt below available liquidity when possible; the ability to repay under severe stress can be more valuable than maximizing purchase size.
- Operational due diligence needs to cover management depth, maintenance capability, raw-material logistics, working capital, and key-person information controls.
- Improved vendor terms can materially improve manufacturing cash conversion, but they do not eliminate cyclicality or execution risk.
- Advice from acquisition influencers should be weighed against their incentives; direct conversations with operators and completed searchers are more grounded inputs.
